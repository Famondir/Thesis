6 Discussion

This chapter consists of the following main sections:

Section 6.1 interprets the results in relation to the research questions and hypotheses, compares them with previous work, and highlights any surprising or unexpected outcomes.

Section 6.2 presents the error analysis and discusses notable findings from a research perspective.

6.1 Research Questions

6.1.1 Page Identification

We posed the following research question and hypotheses for the page identification task:

  1. How can we use LLMs effectively to locate specific information in a financial report?

H1.1: LLMs can be used to locate specific information in a financial report, achieving a high F1 score.

H1.2: LLMs can be combined with other approaches to reduce the energy consumption, without lowering the systems recall.

6.1.1.0.1 Results

The page identification task is solved by the LLM (large language model), achieving higher F1 scores than the human reference for every target class. For the class Aktiva, the task is solved perfectly within our sample. Multi-class classification with Llama 4 Scout yields the best results for Aktiva and Passiva tables, while binary classification with Ministral performs best for GuV tables.

The n_rag_examples prompting strategy frequently produces optimal results, especially when in-company examples (in-context learning examples from the same company as the task’s subject’s company) are used. Section 6.2.2.2 discusses this finding for both tasks.

The term frequency approach achieves a top-k recall of 1.0 for a small \(k = 5\). It operates 100 times faster than the LLM approach and reduces the average number of pages to classify by 92.6%. The combination of both approaches is discussed in Section 6.3.1.4.

6.1.1.0.2 Interpretations

Both hypotheses are supported. Thus, our research question can be answered as follows: It is possible to locate specific information in a financial report using LLMs and efficiency can be further improved by applying a term frequency-based approach for page range refinement in advance.

6.1.1.0.3 Comparison With Previous Work

We are able to narrow the page range to five pages without using a LLM. With the LLM, we are guaranteed to find the correct pages within a range of two pages. Most often, the page ranked highest by confidence score is the correct one. H. Li, Gao, et al. (2023) do not specify the number of pages processed after page refinement.

The TOC approach does not perform as well as expected. H. Li, Gao, et al. (2023) do not report issues or low recall for their TOC-based method. We therefore anticipated a recall close to 1.0, but instead found a recall of 0.27. Differences between the two approaches are discussed in Section 6.3.1.3.

6.1.1.0.4 Unexpected Results

We are surprised by the strong performance of Ministral-8B, which outperforms other, more recent and larger models in the Mistral family. It even demonstrates strong results with the zero_shot and law_context strategies.

We are also positively surprised by the strong performance of the recently released Qwen3 models. The older Qwen3 models could not handle the classification tasks at all — regardless of thinking mode8 — while Qwen2.5 models performed well. The implementation of sparse MoE (mixture of experts) layers is an architectural difference between the newer and older Qwen3 models.

We suspect that the observed performance difference is primarily attributable to variations in the fine-tuning process, rather than architectural changes. While the original models claimed “groundbreaking advancements in […] instruction-following” (Qwen/Qwen3-4B · Hugging Face, 2025), a newer version of Qwen3-4B is now explicitly tagged as Qwen3-4B-Instruction. Including this model in future benchmarks would be interesting.

6.1.1.0.5 Conclusion

In summary, our findings demonstrate that LLMs are highly effective for page identification in financial reports, consistently outperforming human benchmarks and traditional rule-based methods. Combining LLMs with term frequency-based approaches further enhances efficiency, enabling rapid narrowing of candidate pages while maintaining high recall.

Model selection and prompting strategies play a significant role in optimizing performance, with in-context learning using relevant examples yielding the best results. These insights provide a strong foundation for developing robust, scalable solutions for automated document analysis in audit and regulatory contexts.

6.1.2 Information Extraction

We posed the following research question for the information extraction task:

  1. How can we use LLMs effectively to extract this information from the document?

We begin by discussing the hypotheses group H2.1 in Section 6.1.2.1, followed by an examination of hypotheses group H2.2 in Section 6.1.2.2. The hypotheses of group H2.1 are as follows:

H2.1a: LLMs can be used to correctly extract multiple numeric values from the assets table.

H2.1b: LLMs can match row identifiers and place the numeric values in the correct target row.

H2.1c: LLMs can identify unmatched row identifiers and report, that the value is missing.

6.1.2.1 Core Capabilities

6.1.2.1.1 Results

The best-performing model, Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct, nearly matches human performance on real Aktiva tables, while operating much faster. Both metrics — percentage of correct numeric predictions (98.0%) and F1 score (98.1%) — are not yet perfect but could be further improved. The model achieves flawless results on synthetic tables provided in HTML (hyper text markup language) format.

The n_rag_examples prompting strategy frequently yields optimal results, particularly when in-company examples are utilized. Section 6.2.2.2 discusses this finding for both tasks.

6.1.2.1.2 Interpretations

The high percentage of correctly extracted numeric values demonstrates that Qwen3-235B can not only copy values and match corresponding row identifiers precisely, but also perform numeric transformations, respecting currency units in many cases. If no numeric transformations are performed, the upper limit for correct numeric extraction is 80.8%, as 19.2% of all numeric values use T€ as the currency unit. This is discussed in Section 6.2.2.3.

Additionally, for extraction from synthetic Aktiva tables, perfect results are achievable if the input is well-structured and contains no unknown row identifiers. In line with the concept of garbage in, garbage out, this can be phrased as perfect in, perfect out. Thus, there is no inherent mechanism in the LLM approach that prevents perfect extraction of numeric values. This is further illustrated in Appendix 10.2.2.

The high F1 score also indicates that Qwen3-235B can identify missing row identifiers and correctly return null instead of hallucinating numeric values. Label matching is discussed further in Section 6.2.3.

In summary, all hypotheses of group H2.1 are supported, and the research question can be partially answered as follows: LLMs can be used effectively to extract information from financial reports using in-context learning and a strict schema.

6.1.2.1.3 Comparison With Previous Work

H. Li, Gao, et al. (2023) achieved perfect extraction results after refining their approach, extracting 152 data points from 8 ACFR (Annual Comprehensive Financial Report) reports. Before prompt refinement, they found 96.1% correct extracted data points. Applying the refined approach to a more heterogeneous sample of 4_000 county-year ACFRs, they achieved 96% performance on 80_000 data points.

Our observed performance exceeds both their initial in-sample and final out-of-sample results. Since we did not adjust our prompting strategy before the second iteration of ground truth creation, we argue that our performance should be compared to their out-of-sample results.

There are minor differences in our prompting approaches that may influence performance. H. Li, Gao, et al. (2023) include explicit instructions for handling currency units, which we did not for the extraction task with real Aktiva tables. However, we investigated this with the synthetic and hybrid approaches and discuss the findings in Section 6.2.2.3.

H. Li, Gao, et al. (2023) extract three values per LLM call, while we expect 10 to 40 numeric and 18 to 38 null predictions per call. They explicitly instruct how to handle missing values and do not report significantly higher error rates for missing values. Therefore, our F1 score cannot be directly compared to theirs.

6.1.2.1.4 Unexpected Results

We did not anticipate that the LLM would perform currency unit-related numeric transformations without explicit instruction. However, it is plausible that such patterns can be learned during in-context learning, especially when examples are selected based on vector similarity and in-company examples are used. This is described in more detail in Section 6.2.2.2.

6.1.2.1.5 Conclusion

The results demonstrate that LLMs are highly effective for extracting structured financial information from assets tables, nearly matching human performance while operating significantly faster. They can accurately extract multiple numeric values, correctly match row identifiers and reliably identify missing entries.

The success of in-context learning, particularly when in-company examples are used, underscores the importance of prompt design and relevant context. While perfect extraction is achievable with well-structured synthetic data, real-world performance is mainly limited by input quality and schema consistency.

Compared to previous work, the approach presented here achieves higher performance without extensive prompt refinement, highlighting the robustness of the method. Unexpectedly, LLMs can perform currency-related numeric transformations even without explicit instructions, suggesting that in-context learning can capture underlying patterns.

Overall, these findings confirm that LLMs, combined with a strict schema and in-context learning, provide a reliable and scalable solution for automated information extraction in financial reporting.

6.1.2.2 Feature Effects

The hypotheses of group H2.2 are:

H2.2a: Model specific features have an effect on the extraction performance.

H2.2.b: Prompt strategy specific features have an effect on the extraction performance.

H2.2c: Table specific features have an effect on the extraction performance.

6.1.2.2.1 Results

The features most frequently exhibiting notable importance are:

  1. model_family
  2. parameter_count
  3. method_family
  4. n_examples
  5. T_in_previous_year

We can determine the direction of effect for parameter_count, n_examples, and T_in_previous_year: more parameters and more examples are generally beneficial. The only exception is Llama 4 Maverick, discussed in Section 6.2.4. The performance gain diminishes when comparing experiments with three or more provided examples. The prompting strategies n_random_examples and top_n_rag_examples yield the best results.

The method-specific feature respect_units is important for evaluating synthetic tables, but its effect is opposite to our initial assumption. For the hybrid approach, it has a small importance, but the effect direction matches our assumption. This is discussed in the next paragraph.

For synthetic tables, we also find that HTML and Markdown as input_format yield better results. Section 6.3.2 shows that these benefits do not extend to processing real tables from (imperfect) Markdown input.

6.1.2.2.2 Interpretations

All model- and method-specific features are meaningful predictors for the information extraction task. Thus, H2.2a and H2.2b are fully supported. The high importance of model_family may be an artifact of Llama 4 Maverick’s poor performance with multiple in-context learning examples.

Of all table characteristics, only T_in_previous_year shows higher importance. Thus, H2.2c is supported for one of the proposed features. Several features were hypothesized to have no effect and our experiments support this. We assume that T_in_year would show greater importance if more tables with this characteristic were included in our sample.

6.1.2.2.2.1 Respecting Units

We observe opposite effects for the predictor respect_units in information extraction with synthetic tables versus the hybrid approach. This is explained by our experimental designs.

In basic experiments with synthetic tables, extracted values are compared with untransformed gold standard values. The in-context learning examples do not demonstrate numeric transformations, so the LLM only needs to copy digits. This makes the task easier, as no numeric transformation is required.

If the model is instructed to handle currency units, extracted values are compared with transformed gold standard values. The in-context learning examples demonstrate this behavior, making the task harder because the model must learn the numeric transformation pattern.

In contrast, hybrid approach experiments always compare extracted values with transformed gold standard values. The in-context learning examples demonstrate this behavior if the model is instructed to handle currency units, but not otherwise. Thus, the task is easier when the model is instructed to handle currency units, as it can learn the patterns from the in-context examples.

6.1.2.2.3 Compare With Previous Work

H. Li, Gao, et al. (2023) report that the most frequent errors in extracting values from ESG (environmental, social, and governance) reports are due to incorrectly handled units. This aligns with our finding of high importance for T_in_previous_year. Our results also correspond with Brown et al. (2020), who found that performance does not increase substantially after adding more than two examples.

6.1.2.2.4 Limitations

We did not reflect the header_span and text_around characteristics during the creation of synthetic HTML and Markdown tables. Thus, we cannot evaluate the proposed interaction effects with input_format for those features. However, header_span was not found to be important in any experiment.

We also did not include an indicator for in-company examples as a method-related feature, though Section 6.2.2.2 shows its relevance.

6.1.2.2.5 Unexpected Results

We did not expect to find such a clear example of context rot as with Llama 4 Maverick. It is surprising that Llama 4 Scout does not exhibit the same behavior. Since Llama 4 Scout has an even larger context window, it remains to be seen whether context rot will occur if the number of in-context learning examples is increased proportionally.

6.1.2.2.6 Recommendations

We recommend reevaluating the hypotheses for visual table characteristics when more advanced document parsing methods that process visual information are implemented. The document parsing method should be included as a feature in future evaluations.

6.1.2.2.7 Conclusion

Overall, all model and prompt strategy features significantly impact extraction performance, with larger models and well-chosen in-context examples yielding the best results. Table-specific features besides T_in_year play a smaller but sometimes meaningful role. Unlike the classification task, no model family consistently performs worse.

Importantly, providing at least one or two relevant examples is more beneficial than simply increasing model size. These findings highlight the need for improved prompt strategies and model selection to optimize LLM-based information extraction.

6.1.2.3 Error Rate Guidance

We posed the following UX (user experience) inspired side research question and hypotheses:

  1. Can we use additional information from the extraction process to guide the user on which values need to be checked and which can be trusted as they are?

H3.1: The confidence score can be used to guide the user on which of the identified pages need to be checked and which can be trusted as they are.

H3.2: The confidence score can be used to guide the user on which of the predicted values in the information extraction task need to be checked and which can be trusted as they are.

6.1.2.3.1 Results

For the page identification task, we observe high confidence intervals with (near) zero empirical error rates for well-performing models, encompassing most classifications. Ministral-8B exhibits a wide range of confidence scores for the page identification task, which can be used to distinguish between correct and incorrect classifications.

We do not observe high confidence intervals with an error rate below 1% for the information extraction task on real Aktiva tables. Explicitly instructing the model to respect currency units reduces the empirical error rate (see Appendix 10.2.3). However, confidence intervals with zero error rate can be found for the information extraction task on synthetic data.

6.1.2.3.2 Interpretations

We find support for hypothesis H3.1, but not for hypothesis H3.2. Thus, we answer the research question as follows: The confidence score can be used to guide user attention for the page identification task, but is less effective for the information extraction task.

6.1.2.3.3 Compare With Previous Work

The classification task can be interpreted as a best-of-N selection problem with two or four choices. In this context, our simple confidence measure appears sufficient, aligning well with the findings of Kang et al. (2025).

Generating the information extraction response can be viewed as a series of chained best-of-N selection tasks. If every digit, the floating point delimiter and an end-of-sequence token are counted as individual choices, there are 12 possibilities. Deciding whether to return a number or null is an additional binary task, repeated multiple times in this decision chain. Although the number of choices is below \(N=16\) for each decision, chaining these choices seems to produce a less meaningful confidence measure.

6.1.2.3.4 Unexpected Results

Most models predict high confidence for the majority of their predictions, even for incorrect ones.

6.1.2.3.5 Conclusion

In summary, confidence scores are a valuable tool for guiding user attention in the page identification task, reliably indicating which pages require review. However, for information extraction, confidence scores are less effective, as high confidence does not consistently correlate with correct predictions. This underscores the need for more robust uncertainty measures in extraction tasks to better support user decision-making.

6.2 Error Analysis

This section provides a detailed evaluation of key findings from our experimental analysis. The following sections examine classification errors for each method, analyze the challenges of extracting numeric information and discuss the impact of context rot.

Company-specific results and the influence of example selection strategies are presented, followed by a discussion of numeric transformations and the effects of inaccurate text extraction. The chapter also addresses the performance of guided decoding approaches and the process of ground truth creation. Each section aims to highlight practical recommendations and areas for future improvement.

6.2.1 Page Identification

This section presents a brief error analysis for the LLM and term frequency approaches. Errors related to context rot are discussed in Section 6.2.4, as they occurred in both tasks.

6.2.1.1 LLM Approach

The seven misclassified pages by Llama Scout 4 all contain a table occupying at least half a page, with one column of text and many columns of numeric values. Six of these pages are classified as GuV and one as Passiva. The page classified as Passiva contains the term Passiva.

From our perspective, it would be more plausible if page 63 of ‘/pvc/Geschaeftsberichte/Berlinovo/ berlinovo_Finanzbericht_2021.pdf’ were classified as Aktiva, given the presence of many similar row identifiers. The term frequency approach correctly classifies this page as other, ranking it 8th for GuV and Passiva and only 14th for Aktiva. Thus, our intuition regarding the row identifiers in the table was incorrect.

To prevent such misclassifications, the system may require more examples of pages that are similar to the target classes but belong to different types.

6.2.1.2 Term Frequency Approach

Examining some of the pages ranked highly and incorrectly classified as Aktiva by the term frequency-based RF (random forest), we find that the normalized sum of term occurrences can lead to misclassification. For example, one page identified as Aktiva contains no table at all, but rather a long text. The complete term count list based on the Aktiva term list is provided in Appendix 12.8.

The term ‘Geschäfts’ appears 19 times and ‘Unternehmen’ appears 10 times. These terms are likely common throughout the document and should therefore be weighted less. The BM25 measure assigns lower weights to common terms and includes a saturation term, as shown by Kong et al. (2024), who demonstrate that BM25 can be used effectively to identify relevant tables. Using a boolean indicator for term presence on a page, rather than counting individual occurrences, could also be a solution.

We also find pages misclassified due to a high density of floating-point numbers. An example of a page with high float density that does not contain an Aktiva table is shown in Figure 12.5 in the appendix.

We present our practical recommendations in Section 6.3.1.3.1.

6.2.2 Information Extraction

6.2.2.1 General Findings

One of the tested LLM families9 was unable to process German umlauts. Consequently, we replaced these characters with their non-umlaut equivalents and adjusted our schema accordingly.

Overall, the approach does not appear prone to hallucinations. When we inadvertently attempted to extract values from a Passiva or GuV table, no prediction was made, as none of the row identifiers matched our strict schema.

6.2.2.3 Numeric Transformations

6.2.2.3.1 Real tables

This section discusses the unexpectedly high percentage of correctly extracted numeric values. If no numeric transformation is performed, 80.8% is the upper limit. We assume that the tendency to transform numeric values without explicit instruction is not learned during fine-tuning, as the percentage of correct extracted numeric values for the zero_shot (0.763) and static_example (0.803) performances are below the expected limit. The static example demonstrates no numeric transformation and the observed performance is nearly as high as the expected upper limit.

Table 6.1 shows that this limit is exceeded with all other prompting strategies. This is plausible for the top_n_rag_examples strategy when in-company examples are used. It further implies that almost always at least one example among the five random examples demonstrates this transformation and the model recognizes that the pattern shown by a minority of examples applies to the current extraction task. For future work, the provided examples should be logged to investigate this implication.

6.2.2.3.2 Hybrid Approach

We evaluated the impact of providing examples that demonstrate numeric transformation, together with explicit instructions, in the hybrid approach (see Section 9.2.3). The hybrid approach uses synthetic tables as examples for extracting information from real tables. We show that Qwen3-235B can learn to extract numeric values respecting the currency unit and transfer this knowledge from examples with two T€ columns to tables with only one T€ column. This transfer is not possible for all models, e.g., not for Ministral-8B. Some models, such as Qwen3-8B, overgeneralize this pattern and perform worse for extraction subjects without T€ as the currency unit.

Our hybrid approach experiments also reveal that providing real tables as examples, rather than synthetic ones, is more beneficial for extracting numeric values than for matching labels. Comparing Figure 14.29 and Figure 14.30 in the appendix shows that LLMs yield good results for predicting null more consistently than for extracting the correct numeric value when synthetic tables are used as in-context learning examples.

We have not investigated whether an annual report from the following or previous year is more helpful than reports from more distant years. This would be plausible, as it provides correct numeric and missing values for half of the information to extract, perfectly matching our schema formatted as json (JavaScript Object Notation). Theoretically, extracting all values correctly and transforming them according to currency units should become trivial if previous and subsequent years’ annual reports are presented as in-context examples. This should be explored in future work.

6.2.2.4 Inaccurate Text Extracts

We find that the performance of regular expression approaches on the synthetic dataset is highly influenced by the extraction library used. For real data, no difference is observed. In the appendix, Figure 9.1 B shows that the regex (regular expression) approach on texts extracted with pdfium has a wider precision range and a slightly higher number of incorrect numeric values.

A possible reason for this is inaccurate extracted texts. We observe issues such as missing or additional whitespace, misplaced line breaks and extraction of the text column first, followed by numeric columns. Examples of these types of incorrect extracted texts from three different PDF (Portable Document Format) extraction libraries are provided in the Appendix 12.7. One example is from a real table, while two are from synthetic tables.

Random whitespace and line breaks could be addressed by adjusting the regular expression for label matching. Missing whitespace between numeric values could also be handled by modifying the regex. Misplaced columns would require more advanced reformatting strategies. However, these error types should affect recall rather than precision.

A possible explanation for a small spread in precision with both PDF extraction backends is the duplicated row identifier Geleistete Anzahlungen. Our simple approach always matches this row with the first occurrence. If the second occurrence is present in the ground truth but not the first, our approach creates a false positive. The percentage of false positives then determines the precision value and is linked to the total number of rows in the ground truth. However, this pattern is not observed for the pymupdf results.

Another reason for incorrect numeric values is rows where the summed value is given in the next column, but the same row, as the individual values. In this case, the approach selects one individual value and a sum, instead of two individual values.

Originally, synthetic tables were generated with cell lines to improve the performance of classic table extraction libraries. However, we did not use table extraction libraries in our final experimental setup. These lines may confuse pdfium. We also used a zoom function to shrink the table so it fits on a single page, which might negatively affect text extraction quality.

6.2.2.5 OpenAI Models

We were unable to apply a strict schema, either with OpenAI’s closed-weight GPT models or their new open-weight OSS models. Figure 9.3 (see appendix) illustrates that this leads to many responses with few or no predictions whose row identifiers match our ground truth. It is possible that the models simply produced fewer predictions than the expected 29 rows10, a phenomenon observed especially with the nano models. In some cases, certain rows were predicted multiple times, resulting in more than the expected 29 rows.

Figure 9.2 (see appendix) further shows that some responses contain no null values at all, suggesting that the models may hallucinate numeric values. This tendency is again most pronounced in the nano models, but is also observed in the larger GPT-4-1 model when fewer than three in-context learning examples are provided. By contrast, GPT-5-mini and gpt-oss-120B rarely produce responses without any null values.

Nevertheless, even without a strict schema, GPT-4-1 and GPT-5 perform nearly as well as Qwen3-235B. This indicates that a guided decoding approach may be effective too, and that a strict schema is not essential for larger models. Future research should explore the conditions under which open-weight models can be used with guided decoding rather than a strict schema.

Unfortunately, batch processing was not available with the closed-weight models, resulting in runtimes of 30 to 135 minutes. Notably, GPT-5-nano exhibited the longest runtime and generated significantly more output tokens than the other models. We suspect that it may become trapped in repetitive output, exhausting the entire token limit, or operates similarly to the gpt-oss models, which employ the new harmony response format: this format produces many tokens in a chain of thought stream before returning the requested json formatted table string in the final stream. While this may offer insights into the model’s reasoning processes, it substantially increases response time. We would welcome the option to disable the chain of thought stream and receive only the final answer, as is possible with Qwen3.

6.2.2.6 Error Types

A comprehensive analysis of specific error types remains a subject for future research. Here, we briefly outline anticipated error categories, potential evaluation metrics and necessary adjustments to the logging process.

6.2.2.6.1 Numeric Values

For erroneous numeric predictions, it would be valuable to determine whether errors result from copying mistakes — such as omitted or transposed digits — similar to those commonly made by humans. Although we have logged the Levenshtein distance to facilitate this analysis, time constraints prevented further investigation.

Another likely error type involves copying an incorrect value, such as a sum from the same row but a different column, a value from another row or even from one of the provided examples. To examine this, one could check whether the predicted number appears in the prompt text, provided this information is logged.

Errors may also arise from missed or incorrect transformations related to currency units. Additionally, the LLM may perform summations across rows or simply generate hallucinated values, as discussed in Section 6.2.4.

6.2.2.6.2 Row Matching

To analyze false negatives and numeric values assigned to incorrect rows, calculating the Levenshtein distance could be informative; a low distance may indicate the use of a fuzzy matching strategy. Furthermore, semantic similarity scores could be computed to assess whether semantic matching is being employed.

For false positive values, it would be insightful to examine the model’s reasoning process (e.g., Qwen3’s thinking mode or gpt-oss’s chain of thought stream) and to enumerate all omitted rows. While an incomplete list can be generated by reviewing false positives, rows excluded from the ground truth due to schema constraints will remain unaccounted for.

Consequently, a more flexible approach is warranted. This could involve expanding the schema to include a less restrictive category for unmatched rows or adopting a guided decoding strategy. In such cases, we anticipate encountering rows with finer granularity that may need to be aggregated to fit the schema or rows with identifiers that are semantically equivalent but not sufficiently similar for fuzzy matching.

6.2.3 Ground Truth Creation

Manual human work is prone to errors. Therefore, the ground truth creation process would benefit from multiple passes and cross-checks. A strict and detailed coding manual is also essential to ensure consistency throughout the process.

Reviewing the results of LLM predictions enabled us to identify errors in our previously established gold standard. Consequently, we recommend incorporating LLM predictions into the ground truth creation process, especially when a second person is unavailable for cross-checks.

We highlight two findings from our ground truth creation process:

6.2.3.0.1 Page identification

Analysis of the initial experiment predictions revealed valuable insights. Qwen 2.5 consistently classified certain pages as GuV that immediately followed the GuV pages listed in the ground truth. This prediction was correct. The GuV table for the company IBB spans two pages, prompting an adjustment to the ground truth. It now includes all pages for tables that extend across multiple pages.

During the second pass of ground truth creation, we found that 2.4% of the values differed between the previously established gold standard and the results of the second pass. 6.2.4 Context Rot

We observed a decline in performance for Llama 4 Maverick when presented with too many examples in both main tasks. After testing both the FP8 and FP16 versions, we found the same behavior, indicating that low calculation precision is not the cause. Llama 4 Maverick is the only model in our experiments exhibiting the problem of context rot.

Context rot is a term introduced by Kelly Hong & Anton Troynikov (2025), describing a phenomenon in advanced Needle in the Haystack problems, where distractors require LLMs to find semantic similarity rather than exact term matching. They found that accuracy often begins to decrease with input lengths exceeding 10_000 tokens.

Meta claims that Llama 4 Maverick has a context length of 1 million tokens (Llama 4 Scout even 10 million), while other models are typically limited to 128_000, 32_000 or fewer tokens. In most cases, we limited our input token length to 32_000 and reached this limit multiple times. It is notable that Llama 4 Maverick already shows context rot at input lengths of 10_000 tokens — 100 times shorter than its technical context window.

Levy et al. (2024) demonstrated that LLMs’ reasoning performance can degrade at much shorter input lengths than their technical maximum. They also showed that Mistral achieves the highest accuracy when relevant information is positioned at the end of the prompt. However, it remains unclear how these findings apply to our experiments, as we did not include irrelevant information.

6.2.5 Summary

The error analysis compared the performance and limitations of LLM-based and term frequency-based approaches for the page identification and information extraction tasks.

For page identification, LLMs occasionally misclassify tables with similar content as the target classes, while the term frequency method can be misled by common terms and high float density. We recommend improved weighting schemes such as BM25 or boolean indicators.

In information extraction, the use of in-company examples enhances extraction performance. Excessive homogeneity can reduce performance if examples are drawn from documents of other companies. OpenAI models demonstrate that LLMs perform well even without strict schemas, provided their parameter count is sufficiently high.

Overall, the analysis underscores the importance of careful example selection, robust schema design and thorough ground truth validation to minimize errors.

6.3 Practical Implications and Limitations

This section discusses the results from an engineering perspective, focusing on implications for the planned HITL application and potential improvements.

6.3.1 Page Identification

A detailed analysis of the results indicates that a combination of two LLMs may be necessary to achieve optimal performance for each target class. A more general approach would involve using Llama 4 Scout for multi-class classification across all target classes. If available VRAM is limited, Ministral-8B also performs adequately in multi-class classification.

We recommend refining the page range using a term frequency-based approach, as discussed in Section 6.3.1.4, for efficiency. It is essential that any method used for page refinement achieves a recall of 1.0; otherwise, users may need to inspect the entire document, negating any efficiency gains over manual processing.

Subsequently, the LLM can be used to perform multi-class classification on the top k pages identified by the term frequency approach. The page with the highest confidence score from the LLM should be selected for the information extraction task. The confidence score ranking should be retained to present alternative pages if the initial selection is incorrect.

Figure 6.6 illustrates our recommendation to initiate information extraction immediately, without requiring mandatory human confirmation of the selected page. If the user reviews the results and identifies an incorrect page, alternative pages can be manually checked in order of their confidence score ranking.

Pages that have already been classified should be stored in a vector database along with their class labels, enabling reuse in future classification tasks and enhancing overall system performance. For in-context learning, examples should be selected based on vector similarity and should utilize in-company examples. We recommend populating the vector database document by document initially, before transitioning to batch processing.

Figure 6.6: Showing the information extraction process in a HITL application. We propose to include user action only after the information extraction. If a wrong page is selected, this can be fixed and extraction runs again. Wrong extracted values and handling unknown row identifiers should be done in one place.

6.3.1.1 Transferring the System to New Problems

Expanding the system to new application areas should be straightforward, even for non-expert users. For the term frequency approach, a pipeline can be established where users input a list of keywords and receive a page ranking based on term frequency metrics. Users can refine the keyword list or directly select relevant pages to create a ground truth. If additional metrics are needed (such as float frequency), the system can automatically train a RF classifier.

Alternatively, users can provide documents along with a list indicating which pages contain the desired information. This data can serve as the foundation for a retrieval-augmented few-shot classifier, which will improve as more pages are classified.

6.3.1.2 Limitations of Transfer

The term frequency and LLM classification may perform less effectively if the information of interest constitutes only a small portion of the page content. If the information is presented in a table, a visual table detection model can be used to identify all tables. As shown in Appendix 12.3.2.2, this is a promising approach. The retrieval-augmented few-shot approach can then be used to identify the correct table.

If the information is embedded in regular sentences rather than tables, it may be challenging to locate the correct page using this approach. In such cases, the TOC approach could be employed for page range refinement if the information is found in a section with a known heading.

6.3.1.3 Possible Improvement

6.3.1.3.1 Term Frequency Approach

Based on the error analysis for the term frequency approach (see Section 6.2.1.2), we recommend using either the sum of boolean indicators or the sum of BM25 scores instead of the normalized sum of term frequencies. Alternatively, each individual indicator could be used as a feature for the RF classifier; however, this would require a larger training and test dataset to avoid overfitting due to the increased number of features.

6.3.1.3.2 Table of Contents Understanding Approach

H. Li, Gao, et al. (2023) applied a few-shot learning strategy for the TOC approach, whereas we implemented only a zero-shot strategy. With greater expertise and more concrete examples included in the prompt, this method would likely achieve better results, as reported in Section 5.1.2. Additionally, they used OpenAI’s GPT-4 for the TOC understanding task, which has significantly more parameters than Ministral-8B. It would be worthwhile to investigate whether Qwen3-235B performs better with text-based TOC.

6.3.1.4 Energy Usage and Runtime

The fastest and least energy-consuming strategy, using only LLMs, is to use a small model such as Ministral-8B-Instruct for the multi-class approach. This is more effective than running three binary classifications.

An alternative approach could involve first predicting whether a page is of any target type using binary classification, followed by determining the exact type. However, this would likely consume as much energy as the multi-class approach, since a balanced number of examples must be provided for each class. The results of the multi-class strategy are sufficient to justify its direct use.

In both strategies, the k required for perfect recall is three, using the Ministral-8B-Instruct model11.

Nevertheless, it is more promising to reduce the number of pages to be classified by the LLM in the first place. This can be achieved by running the term frequency approach first to refine the page range, followed by the LLM approach.

6.3.1.4.1 Comparison With Manual Page Identification

The manual approach is the slowest. We identified the pages of interest for all target classes in ten random documents for the benchmark, using the TOC and the search function to find keywords such as Aktiva or Bilanz. Nevertheless, it is almost as fast as the full multi-classification using Llama 4 Scout, while consuming eight times less energy. Compared to Ministral-8B-Instruct, it takes three times longer but consumes less than half the energy.

Thus, when comparing full manual processing and full LLM-driven classification, a key advantage of the LLM approach is that the human user can perform other tasks while the LLM classifies a batch of documents, freeing the user from repetitive work. However, in terms of energy consumption, the manual approach remains more efficient.

Factors not yet considered in this comparison include:

  • Costs to purchase and maintain hardware (e.g., a GPU (graphics processing unit) cluster).
  • Higher costs per runtime/token if LLM compute is purchased from cloud providers.
  • Payment and insurance costs for human workers (e.g., student assistants).
  • Training time and energy consumption for training either
    • a LLM (typically performed by the LLM provider).
    • a human (education and development).
  • Energy consumed to produce the hardware.

The most important factor for many CEOs is likely cost. The costs presented in Table 5.3 include only energy costs.

To calculate cloud computing costs, it is important to know how many tokens are processed. The number of response tokens per page identified can be limited to one and is therefore negligible. In contrast, the number of input tokens required to classify a single page ranges from \(10^3\) to \(10^4\) tokens, depending on the number of examples provided for in-context learning. For example:

  • Binary classification with 3 random examples: 6.5k input tokens
  • Multi-class classification with 3 random examples: 11k input tokens

Assuming GPT-4.1-mini hosted in the Azure cloud is used instead of running the LLM locally, we estimate a price of 118.80 € for multi-class classification of 1_000 documents, assuming a cost of $0.4 per 1M input tokens and 27 pages per document. For a human employee, payment and insurance costs must be added to energy costs. Even for a student worker, this amounts to 0.5 cents per second, totaling 319.80 € for identifying target pages in 1_000 documents (assuming 15 € per hour and 10% additional insurance costs).

This comparison is valid only if the software for automated document processing is already available. Otherwise, development costs must also be considered, which are difficult to estimate and depend on the required software quality. For example, hiring a software developer at a rate of 80 € per hour for 160 hours would add 12.80 € per document for a batch of 1_000 documents, or 12_800 € in total. Estimating my own development time for this project at 1_400 hours and a salary of 50 € per hour, the total cost would amount to 70_000 €. Notably, the software is still not fully completed.

6.3.1.5 Conclusion

In summary, combining term frequency-based page refinement with LLM-driven multi-class classification provides an efficient and accurate approach for page identification in financial documents. Storing classified pages in a vector database and leveraging in-context learning further enhances system performance and adaptability.

While limitations exist when relevant information is sparse or embedded in non-tabular text, integrating visual table detection and retrieval-augmented strategies can address these challenges.

Regarding energy usage and runtime, our analysis demonstrates that smaller LLMs such as Ministral-8B-Instruct offer a highly efficient solution for multi-class page identification, outperforming manual methods in speed and energy consumption. While manual identification remains less resource-intensive, it is significantly slower and less scalable.

When hardware, cloud computing, and personnel costs are considered, automated LLM-based approaches offer substantial economic advantages, especially for large-scale document processing. Prioritizing page range refinement before LLM classification further optimizes resource use, making the system both cost-effective and environmentally responsible.

If development costs are considered as well, manual processing may be more economical for small batches of documents. However, as development costs are amortized over a large number of documents, the automated approach becomes increasingly favorable. This is particularly true when considering the potential for future applications of the developed software. However, organizations must carefully evaluate their specific needs and resources to determine the most suitable method for their document processing tasks.

6.3.2 Information Extraction

It remains unclear whether the 2% of incorrectly extracted numeric values are due to unhandled currency units or other factors. One possible explanation is that faulty text extraction may cause numeric values to be concatenated. If so, it raises the question of whether further investment in advanced table extraction methods is warranted.

To further assess extraction performance, we evaluated text extracts from various Python libraries, including advanced solutions such as Azure Document Intelligence and Docling, both capable of table detection and extraction. We also experimented with providing Markdown instead of plain text — generated by Docling and pymupdf — to preserve table structure information and tested an OCR (optical character recognition)-based approach using tesseract.

Table 6.3 presents aggregated results for the best prompting method with Qwen3-235B across all input types. No improvement was observed over results obtained with text extracted by pdfium. In fact, performance declined when using Markdown generated by pymupdf or text produced via OCR. Notably, Qwen3-235B performed equally well with Markdown generated by Docling as with other plain text extracts, indicating that the issue is not specific to the Markdown format.

Therefore, we advocate prioritizing the development of end-to-end data pipelines to eliminate the need for document parsing altogether.

6.3.2.0.1 Limitations

The current approach uses a single strict schema, enabling straightforward evaluation of results and downstream processing. However, this excludes reports from well-known companies such as BVG and BSR. For the final system, we intend to add an additional classifier between the page identification and information extraction steps to detect the granularity at which the asset table is reported. A suitable schema can then be applied in the information extraction task.

This will still not utilize all information found in all tables. Therefore, we plan to identify which row identifiers do not match and extract the corresponding rows in a separate json-formatted list. We need to test whether this can be performed in a single step or requires an additional information extraction prompt.

Another approach would be to use guided rather than restricted decoding. In this strategy, the description of the target structure is included in the prompt, and only the generation of valid json code is enforced. We inadvertently conducted initial tests with this approach, as we were unable to use a strict schema with OpenAI models. Our findings are described in Section 6.2.2.5.

6.3.2.0.2 Conclusion

In summary, we recommend investing resources in end-to-end provision of machine-readable information and user experience, rather than striving for perfect extraction results. We believe that current performance is already sufficient, especially if the document extraction database is built document by document to leverage a RAG (retrieval augmanted generation) architecture and in-context learning with in-company examples.

6.3.3 Error Rate Guidance

Implementing a measure to guide users’ attention, indicating which predictions require review and which can be trusted, would be highly beneficial for the information extraction task. Verifying the extracted values for a single table can take up to three minutes, amounting to 300 minutes for the entire sample. This process is up to four times faster than manually transferring all values.

Choosing a smaller model that completes in 2:30 minutes rather than 3:00 minutes does not significantly accelerate the workflow, as error checking must still be performed on all values. Error checking could be omitted if perfect results are achieved or if a reliable criterion is identified that allows predictions to be grouped into bins with a near-zero error rate.

One promising criterion is the company that published the document. As demonstrated in 6.2.2.2, information extraction already yields error-free results for many companies’ documents. If the table structure remains consistent, similar empirical error rates can be expected for future documents. However, some companies in our sample have introduced structural changes, such as reporting values in T€, which affect error rates.

Additionally, we recommend selecting a threshold value close to zero, rather than exactly zero, to define groups of predictions with sufficiently low empirical error rates. Otherwise, these groups may become too unstable.

More sophisticated confidence measures could also be explored, but we are not optimistic that they will provide substantially better discrimination.

6.3.3.0.1 Conclusion

Overall, the system effectively distinguishes between correctly and incorrectly classified pages, enabling the creation of a ranking that helps users quickly identify the most promising alternatives when misclassifications occur. Although this functionality may be seldom required due to the high precision achieved, it provides a valuable safety net for edge cases.

For information extraction, further segmentation could enhance the utility of confidence scores, but alternative grouping criteria may ultimately prove more effective and could reduce reliance on confidence scores altogether.

6.4 Not Covered

This section briefly outlines topics that were not addressed within the scope of this thesis.

6.4.0.0.1 Table Extraction and Document Parsing

Classical libraries and modern machine learning models are available for table extraction from documents. We evaluated tabula as a traditional solution; however, its performance was unsatisfactory — particularly for tables without borders, where it frequently failed to correctly identify rows and columns.

We were unable to implement more recent models for this task, such as visual LLMs. Although promising results have been reported for these approaches, user-friendly implementations for transformers or vLLM remain limited. Notably, our method performed well using basic text extraction, so we did not pursue more advanced extraction tools or models further.

6.4.0.0.2 Multi-page Tables

Methods for handling multi-page tables were not investigated, as such tables were only encountered for GuV tables. Addressing this will be a software engineering challenge for the final system. Document layout analysis models could be used to identify related pages, allowing text extracts to be concatenated for subsequent information extraction.

6.4.0.0.3 Optical Character Recognition

For documents consisting solely of scanned images, OCR is an essential preprocessing step, as all approaches in this thesis rely on text extraction. OCR systems typically include document layout analysis to manage multi-column layouts and tables.

6.4.0.0.4 Fine-tuning

LLMs can be fine-tuned for specific tasks, such as generating text in a particular style or performing classification. For classification, a softmax pooling layer is added. The object detection model Yolo 12 used in this work is an example of a fine-tuned model for table detection.

We did not perform fine-tuning with LLMs, opting instead for in-context learning to assess its sufficiency. It would be valuable to estimate how many in-context prompts are required before the energy consumption for additional token processing exceeds that of fine-tuning.

6.4.0.0.5 Training an Encoder-only Model

Training a smaller encoder-only model, such as BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers), may be more efficient for classification tasks. However, unlike LLMs, such models would need to be retrained for each new classification task, necessitating the creation of a dedicated training dataset.

6.4.0.0.6 UX Design Study

A UX design study has not yet been conducted. Such a study could be valuable in the near future to develop an application based on the information extraction processes explored in this thesis, tailored for employees of RHvB. Early involvement of potential users is beneficial for successful software development (User Participation in Software Development Projects – Communications of the ACM, 2010), helping to avoid unnecessary features and cumbersome workflows, while also increasing motivation and willingness to adopt the final AI-driven product (Errida & Lotfi, 2021).

6.4.0.0.7 Advanced Prompting Techniques

Advanced prompting strategies, such as Chain-of-Thought, ReAct or Self-Consistency (Prompt Engineering Guide, n.d.), encourage step-by-step reasoning and deeper effort. However, given the strong performance already achieved and initial signs of context rot, further investment in advanced prompt template development is not recommended at this stage.

6.5 Ethical & Practical Considerations

6.5.1 Ethical Considerations

Extracting numeric information is distinct from decision-making and is unlikely to introduce human-discriminating bias. Automation of information extraction may replace roles requiring lower qualifications; however, at RHvB, such positions no longer exist. Thus, this automation primarily serves to enhance efficiency, allowing staff to focus on more complex tasks.

According to current regulations, individuals should not be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing (such as algorithms) that are legally binding or significantly affect them. However, the AI Act is likely not applicable here, as no decisions are made at the individual level.

Furthermore, errors that could influence decisions should be caught by an employee in the HITL approach before they have downstream implications.

6.5.2 Computational Constraints

Information extraction using LLMs is computationally intensive and ideally requires GPU acceleration. For example, running Qwen3-235B-A32B-Instruct-FP8, the model yielding the best extraction results, requires four H200 GPUs. Similarly, Llama 4 Scout, which performed best in page identification, also needs four H200 GPUs. Loading these models ad hoc is impractical due to setup times of approximately 30 minutes each. In contrast, Ministral-8B and Qwen3-8B can operate on a single NVIDIA 5090 RTX or A100.

6.5.3 Generalizability Scope

The approach evaluated here is likely applicable to annual reports from other companies. However, if the information of interest occupies only a small portion of a page, the framework may require adaptation. Page identification may also become more challenging when only a single keyword is available for search.

6.5.4 PDF Extraction Limitations

Pdfminer indicates that the text from certain annual reports, such as those from IBB and Berlinovo, should not be extracted, as noted in the PDF metadata. Nevertheless, we utilized these text extracts for our study.

6.6 Outlook

To implement the proposed system at RHvB, several infrastructural challenges must be addressed. With the transition away from local infrastructure, migration to a secure cloud environment is required, including establishing protected connections to the Berlin intranet and ensuring confidential data is processed securely. In the short term, the strategy does not yet incorporate GPU resources.

Key areas for improvement include enhancing system flexibility and expanding the range of supported use cases. This involves developing solutions for users to manage unknown row identifiers and automating the process of populating the RAG component with examples for identification and extraction tasks.

A user interface must be developed to guide users through the workflow, enabling error correction and the handling of unknown row identifiers. Additionally, procedures for managing new entries need to be defined: whether to aggregate them with existing row identifiers, extend the database of known entities or introduce a new hierarchy level to accommodate more granular reporting by housing cooperatives.

We also plan to establish a machine learning operations pipeline to monitor system health and facilitate ongoing benchmarking. This will allow for systematic evaluation of new models and, crucially, assessment of whether new examples provided by employees may negatively impact system performance.

Furthermore, we intend to request annual reports in XBRL (eXtensible Bbusiness Reporting Language) format. Since 2012, German companies have been required to submit reports in this machine-readable format for tax purposes (§ 5b EStG), although RHvB has not yet utilized this resource. Our goal is to develop a robust, error-free solution for this specific task based on XBRL data.

6.7 Summary

This chapter critically evaluated the performance, limitations and practical implications of LLMs and term frequency-based approaches for page identification and information extraction in financial documents. The error analysis highlighted strengths and weaknesses of both methods, emphasizing the importance of robust schema design, careful example selection and thorough ground truth validation.

Practical recommendations are provided for system implementation, including the integration of HITL workflows, energy and cost considerations and strategies for expanding the system to new use cases. Ethical and computational constraints are discussed, with attention to resource requirements and the impact of automation on the workforce.

The chapter concludes that combining term frequency-based refinement with LLM-driven classification offers an efficient, scalable and adaptable solution, while also outlining areas for future improvement and topics not covered in the current work.
